A young man was brought unconscious into a Florida hospital in 1980. His family,
speaking Spanish, used the word <span lang="es">intoxicado</span>.

<blockquote data-specimen>
  <p><span lang="es">intoxicado</span> &nbsp;→&nbsp; intoxicated</p>
</blockquote>

In the family's usage the word means poisoned, or made ill by something
swallowed. It was taken in its English-looking sense. He was treated for a drug
overdose. He had suffered a brain haemorrhage, and by the time it was found he
was quadriplegic.

## Why it is in this corpus

Because the mechanism is the plainest in the collection and the outcome is the
worst. A false friend is a lookup failure with no table involved: two words that
resemble each other across languages and do not mean the same thing.

There was no interpreter. That is the actual mechanism, and it is the same shape
as the Swansea sign — a process in which no step required a competent reader.

## Why it is secondary, and why that matters here

The case is widely reported and consistently described, and this course has read
the reporting rather than the record. The figure usually attached to the
settlement comes from that same secondary reporting.

For a menu, secondary is a note. For this, it is a limit on what anyone in this
course is entitled to assert, and week 9's bench is partly about the difference.
