The story goes that an early machine translation system was given

<blockquote data-specimen>
  <p>The spirit is willing but the flesh is weak</p>
</blockquote>

translated it into Russian, translated it back, and produced

<blockquote data-specimen>
  <p>The vodka is good but the meat is rotten</p>
</blockquote>

## This entry is apocryphal, and that is its function

No account of this story names the system that produced it. None gives a date
beyond a decade. None cites a person who was in the room, and the round-trip
procedure it describes was not a normal way to demonstrate a translation system
in the period it is attributed to.

It is held here as apocryphal: widely repeated, no primary source found. The
course does not drop it, because a corpus that quietly deletes what it cannot
source teaches the opposite of what this one is for.

## What would change this record

A contemporaneous document — a report, a memo, a photograph of a printout —
naming the system and the date. Week 3's bench exists to look for one. Every
year the chain has stopped in the same place, and that stopping point is itself
the finding.

## Why it survives anyway

Because it is a good joke and because it flatters the teller: it says that
machines are stupid in a way people are not. That is worth noticing in a room
full of people about to write down what they think a machine did.
